Class IV laser therapy is sometimes presented as though the Roman numeral were a clinical grade. It is not. In laser safety standards, the class describes potential hazard from the light—not the quality or effectiveness of a treatment.

People searching for Class 4 laser therapy in physical therapy usually want to know whether the higher classification means a better treatment. It does not. The more useful questions are what kind of light is delivered, at what dose, for which condition, and what outcome the research actually measured.

## What Class IV laser therapy actually is

Class IV devices produce high-power laser light. Their output can injure unprotected eyes and may harm skin if used improperly, so eye protection, application technique, and treatment parameters must be controlled.

A higher safety class does not mean a deeper repair or stronger clinical result. It means the device can produce more optical power than lower-level lasers used in much of the earlier photobiomodulation research.

Power listed on a device is not the same as the dose delivered to a particular tissue. Wavelength, power, treatment time, beam area, application method, skin characteristics, and other factors all influence exposure.

Much of the photobiomodulation literature involves lower-level laser devices or light-emitting diodes. Their wavelengths and dosing protocols may differ from those used by a Class IV device. Findings cannot automatically be transferred from one device or protocol to another. A meaningful interpretation of “laser research” requires knowing the condition, device, wavelength, dose, and comparison treatment involved.

## A biological signal—not a repair claim

Photobiomodulation is generally described as using red or near-infrared light to influence biological signaling. Laboratory models suggest that certain wavelengths may interact with components of cellular energy metabolism and affect signaling related to blood flow and inflammation.

These proposed mechanisms are not the same as a clinical outcome. A therapist does not measure mitochondrial activity, adenosine triphosphate production, or tissue regeneration during a routine treatment session.

Clinical trials usually measure outcomes such as pain, stiffness, range of motion, and function over days or weeks. A change in pain or movement does not establish that cartilage, tendon, muscle, or a spinal disc has been structurally repaired.

The most accurate interpretation is that light may provide a biological stimulus. Whether that produces a useful change depends on the condition, dose, individual response, and broader rehabilitation plan.

## What the evidence suggests

The evidence varies by condition, and results from one diagnosis should not be applied automatically to another.

### Knee osteoarthritis

Research suggests photobiomodulation may provide short-term pain relief for some people with knee osteoarthritis. Results vary by protocol, and symptom improvement does not mean that joint degeneration has been reversed.

### Tendon pain and neck pain

Evidence ranges from limited to moderate depending on the specific condition, device, and treatment protocol. When laser is used, it is generally best considered an adjunct to exercise, load management, and an appropriate rehabilitation plan rather than a stand-alone solution.

### Low back pain

Evidence remains mixed. Studies use different devices, doses, treatment combinations, and patient populations, making strong conclusions difficult. Laser should not replace an assessment of the factors contributing to the pain.

Across these conditions, trials primarily report pain and function. They do not establish that Class IV laser repairs or regenerates tissue. Findings from lower-power devices should not be attributed to a high-power device unless the treatment parameters and clinical context are reasonably comparable.

## How laser differs from heat and ultrasound

Heat packs primarily warm the skin and superficial tissues. They may feel soothing and temporarily reduce stiffness, but the effect often fades after the heat is removed.

Therapeutic ultrasound uses sound waves rather than light. It may produce thermal and nonthermal effects, but research has not consistently shown meaningful, lasting benefits for many common musculoskeletal conditions when compared with active care.

Class IV laser is a different modality because it delivers light rather than heat or sound. Higher-power laser application can also create noticeable warmth, however, which matters both for safety and for interpreting the response.

If symptoms improve after a session, it may be difficult to isolate the contribution of light from warmth, clinician contact, natural symptom variation, expectation, and the rest of the treatment plan. For most musculoskeletal conditions, progressive movement, loading, and return to meaningful activity remain the foundation. A modality, when used, should support that plan rather than replace it.

## What a session may feel like

The patient and clinician wear appropriate eye protection throughout the application. The laser applicator is typically moved over the treatment area according to the device protocol rather than held indefinitely over one point.

Many people describe mild warmth. The experience varies with the device, settings, treatment area, and application method. Burning, sharp pain, or excessive heat should be reported immediately so the application can be stopped and reassessed.

A practical approach is to define meaningful outcomes before treatment begins—for example, walking tolerance, lifting, sleep, range of motion, or a specific work task—and reassess them after a limited trial. If there is no meaningful improvement, continuing repeated sessions may not be justified.

There is no universal number of sessions that is appropriate for every diagnosis or every patient.

## Who it may fit—and who should be assessed first

Laser may be considered as an optional adjunct when the clinical problem has been evaluated, relevant precautions have been reviewed, and treatment is part of an active rehabilitation plan. It is not a substitute for diagnosis, progressive exercise, or medical evaluation when warning signs are present.

Laser safety requires strict eye protection and careful screening of the treatment area. Pregnancy, known or suspected malignancy in the treatment field, photosensitizing medications, altered sensation, skin conditions, and other individual factors may require avoidance, modification, or medical consultation depending on the circumstances and device guidance.

Symptoms such as progressive weakness or numbness, changes in bowel or bladder control, fever, a hot swollen joint, unexplained weight loss, severe symptoms after significant trauma, or other concerning changes warrant appropriate medical assessment before beginning a clinic modality.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Class IV laser the same as cold laser?

No. “Cold laser” commonly refers to lower-power devices that produce little or no noticeable heat. Class IV devices can deliver substantially more power, so warmth may be felt and laser-specific eye protection is essential. The treatments share the broader concept of photobiomodulation, but their devices and dosing may differ.

### Does laser therapy hurt?

It is generally intended to be comfortable, and mild warmth is commonly reported. Burning, sharp pain, or excessive heat is not an expected treatment goal and should be reported immediately.

### How many sessions will I need?

There is no reliable universal number. The plan depends on the diagnosis, treatment parameters, response, and functional goals. A short, defined trial with reassessment is more informative than committing in advance to an open-ended series.

### Can laser replace exercise?

No. Laser does not rebuild strength, movement capacity, or tolerance for daily activity by itself. When it is used, it should complement rather than replace an appropriate active rehabilitation program.
