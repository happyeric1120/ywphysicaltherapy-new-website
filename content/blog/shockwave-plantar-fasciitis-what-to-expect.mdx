Months of heel pain are frustrating, especially when every morning starts with painful first steps. You may have already tried basic care without getting enough relief. Before considering shockwave therapy, it helps to understand what the problem may be and what this treatment can and cannot do.

## What plantar fasciitis actually is

Plantar fasciitis is often better understood as plantar fasciopathy: an overload-related problem affecting the fascia where it attaches at the heel. The tissue may show thickening and changes in collagen organization rather than a purely inflammatory process. That distinction matters because treatment is usually aimed at managing load and improving function, not inflammation alone.

A classic symptom is pain with the first steps in the morning. The fascia rests overnight, and the first load of the day can produce sharp pain beneath the heel. Many people improve over time with footwear changes, activity modification, and an appropriate loading program. When symptoms persist for several months, the condition may be described as chronic.

Heel spurs are common in people both with and without heel pain. A spur is not necessarily the source of symptoms, and shockwave therapy does not remove it.

## How shockwave therapy works

Shockwave therapy delivers mechanical pulses through a device placed against the skin near the painful area. Two commonly used forms are focused and radial shockwave. Focused shockwave directs energy toward a selected depth, while radial shockwave produces pressure waves that are strongest near the applicator and spread through more superficial tissue.

Research has not established one approach as universally superior. The appropriate technology and treatment parameters depend on the clinical presentation and the treating clinician's judgment.

Shockwave provides a mechanical stimulus that may influence blood flow, nerve sensitivity, and cellular signaling. Clinical studies primarily measure changes in pain and function; they do not establish that shockwave regenerates or repairs plantar fascia tissue. Shockwave is not ultrasound or laser therapy, does not remove heel spurs, and does not guarantee a cure.

## What the evidence says

Research suggests that shockwave therapy may improve pain and function in some people with chronic plantar fasciopathy, particularly when symptoms have continued despite conservative care. Results vary between studies because treatment protocols, patient selection, outcome measures, and definitions of success differ.

For that reason, we do not publish a single success rate. A percentage taken from one study may not accurately predict what any individual patient will experience.

Many clinical trials use a short series of treatments, commonly around three sessions spaced approximately one week apart, although protocols vary. Outcomes are often evaluated several weeks after the treatment course. Some people notice changes earlier, while others may not experience meaningful improvement.

Temporary effects can include discomfort during treatment, soreness, redness, swelling, bruising, or altered sensation. More serious complications appear to be uncommon in appropriately selected patients, but individual risks should be reviewed before treatment. Shockwave is one possible option within a broader plan of care, not a guaranteed solution.

## What to expect, session by session

### Before the first session

A physical therapist evaluates the symptoms, examines the foot and lower leg, and considers whether another condition could be contributing to the heel pain. The clinician also reviews relevant medical history, precautions, goals, and expectations before deciding whether shockwave is appropriate.

Treatment can be uncomfortable while the pulses are applied, and improvement is not expected to occur immediately after the first session. Treatment intensity and the number of sessions are individualized rather than guaranteed in advance.

### Session 1

Gel is applied to the skin, and the applicator delivers pulses around the identified treatment area. The sensation may feel like strong tapping or a deep ache. Treatment time and intensity depend on the clinical plan.

Most people can walk afterward, although temporary soreness may occur. Redness, swelling, bruising, or altered sensation are also possible. How intense the treatment feels does not indicate whether it will be effective.

### Sessions 2–3

Later sessions generally follow a similar process. Some people notice gradual changes during the treatment series, while others notice little difference until several weeks after it is completed. A common research protocol uses approximately three sessions, but the appropriate schedule may differ.

### After the treatment course

If the heel is temporarily more sensitive, high-impact activity may need to be reduced for a short period. Walking may still be appropriate, while returning to running and jumping should be gradual and based on symptoms and clinical guidance.

Progress is evaluated over time using changes in pain, walking tolerance, morning symptoms, and function. If symptoms do not change meaningfully, the diagnosis and overall plan should be reconsidered rather than assuming that repeated courses will necessarily produce a result.

## Who may be considered—and who may need another approach

Shockwave may be considered for selected adults with persistent plantar heel pain when appropriate conservative measures have not provided enough improvement. It is not suitable for every person or every cause of heel pain.

An assessment should consider other possible causes, including a calcaneal stress injury, infection or open wound, plantar fascia rupture, nerve-related pain, inflammatory conditions, or unexplained night pain and systemic symptoms. Heel spurs alone are not the treatment target.

Pregnancy, implanted electronic devices, anticoagulant medication, active malignancy in the treatment area, open growth plates, recent injections, and other individual medical factors require careful screening. Do not stop or change prescribed medication for shockwave treatment without guidance from the prescribing clinician.

## Frequently asked questions

### Will it hurt?

It can be uncomfortable. Pulses may feel like strong tapping or a deep ache, and temporary soreness afterward is possible. The amount of discomfort does not establish whether the treatment is working.

### How many sessions will I need?

Research protocols commonly use around three sessions spaced approximately one week apart, sometimes more. There is no single standardized dose or schedule that is appropriate for everyone, so the plan should be based on an individual assessment.

### When might I feel better?

Improvement, when it occurs, is generally measured over weeks rather than immediately after the first session. Some people notice changes within four to six weeks, while research outcomes are often assessed around 12 weeks. Individual responses vary.

### Can I walk and work the same day?

Many people can walk and continue ordinary daily activities after a session. Temporary soreness may affect more demanding activity, and returning to running or jumping should be gradual.

### Can the pain return?

It can. Shockwave has not been shown to prevent recurrence. Activity load, footwear, strength, mobility, and the way you return to activity may still matter after treatment.
