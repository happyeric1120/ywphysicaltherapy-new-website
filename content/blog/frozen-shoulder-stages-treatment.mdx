Frozen shoulder has a frustrating pattern. Pain often appears first, stiffness becomes more noticeable, and ordinary tasks such as reaching a shelf or fastening clothing behind your back become difficult. Advice can sound contradictory, making it hard to know whether to move the arm more or give it time to settle.

The answer often depends on which problem is dominant now: pain, stiffness, or the gradual return of movement.

## What frozen shoulder actually is

Frozen shoulder, also called adhesive capsulitis, is a condition in which the capsule surrounding the shoulder joint becomes painful and loses its normal mobility. As the capsule tightens, the joint cannot move through its usual range.

Both active motion—what you can do yourself—and passive motion—what someone else can move for you—are typically restricted. External rotation, or turning the arm outward with the elbow at your side, is often one of the most limited movements.

The course varies and can last many months. The commonly described stages overlap and do not follow a fixed calendar. They are best used as a guide for matching care to the shoulder's current irritability rather than predicting an exact recovery date.

## Stage 1: The painful or freezing stage

During the freezing stage, pain is usually the dominant problem while stiffness is developing. A deep shoulder ache and night pain are common. Lying on the affected side, dressing, reaching, or lifting even a light object may become increasingly uncomfortable.

Movement is generally kept gentle and tolerable. Comfortable pendulum movements, pain-limited range-of-motion work, and light daily use may help maintain movement without repeatedly aggravating the shoulder. Forceful end-range stretching can increase symptoms when the joint is highly irritable.

This stage is often described as lasting several months, but the duration varies considerably. Strong night pain and increasing stiffness can occur at the same time, and repeated symptom flares may make progress feel uneven.

The early goal is usually to manage pain, protect sleep, and preserve tolerable movement—not to force the shoulder to full range. Escalating pain or an inability to rest comfortably warrants an assessment so other causes of shoulder pain are not overlooked.

## Stage 2: The frozen or stiff stage

As the condition progresses, pain at rest may ease while stiffness becomes the main limitation. Active and passive motion remain restricted. External rotation, overhead reach, and reaching behind the back are commonly difficult.

Daily activities often reveal the restriction: washing or styling hair, fastening clothing, pulling a seatbelt across the body, or reaching overhead may feel blocked even when the sharper pain has settled.

Progressive mobility work generally becomes more prominent during this stage. The aim is steady improvement rather than one forceful stretch. A tolerable stretch performed consistently is often more useful than repeatedly provoking a flare. If soreness increases substantially or persists into the next day, the dose may need to be adjusted.

Light strengthening and use of the arm within the available range may also help maintain shoulder-girdle capacity. The length of this stage varies from person to person.

## Stage 3: The thawing or recovery stage

Movement tends to return gradually, often over months rather than weeks. Overhead reach and external rotation may be among the slower motions to recover. Pain is usually milder and more connected to activities that demand range or load the shoulder has not yet regained.

The goal now expands from mobility to strength and function. Rotator-cuff and shoulder-blade strength, confidence using the arm, and tolerance for reaching and lifting all need to be rebuilt gradually.

A flare does not necessarily mean recovery has stopped, but it may indicate that the stretching or loading dose was too high. The plan can be scaled back and progressed again as symptoms settle.

Some people retain a degree of motion loss after recovery. The practical goal is useful, comfortable movement for the activities that matter—not necessarily a perfect match to the other shoulder.

## Why timing changes the treatment plan

Frozen shoulder does not require the same plan from beginning to end.

In a pain-dominant stage, care generally emphasizes symptom management, sleep, and gentle pain-limited motion. Complete immobilization is usually not the goal, but forceful stretching of a highly irritable joint can be counterproductive.

When stiffness dominates, progressive stretching and mobility work become more important. The appropriate duration, intensity, and frequency depend on how the shoulder responds rather than on a rigid protocol.

During the thawing stage, improving motion needs to be paired with strength and functional activity so the shoulder can again tolerate reaching, carrying, lifting, and daily work.

Stage labels are guides rather than strict dates. A symptom flare can temporarily shift care back toward a pain-dominant approach even later in the course. That is why [shoulder pain care](/shoulder-pain-nyc) should be matched to how the shoulder presents now, not only to a stage name.

## Where shockwave and laser may fit—and where they do not

Evidence for shockwave and laser specifically for frozen shoulder remains limited and mixed. They are best considered optional adjuncts rather than primary treatments.

In selected cases, an adjunct may help reduce symptoms enough for a person to participate more comfortably in a mobility program. Clinical studies generally measure pain and function; they do not establish that shockwave or laser releases the capsule, repairs it, or shortens a particular stage.

Neither treatment replaces stage-appropriate movement and progressive rehabilitation. If an adjunct does not produce a meaningful functional benefit, simply adding more sessions may not improve the underlying mobility limitation.

Corticosteroid injection is a medical procedure that may provide short-term pain relief for some patients, particularly earlier in a pain-dominant course. Hydrodilatation, manipulation under anesthesia, and arthroscopic capsular release are other medical options sometimes considered for selected cases. Their appropriateness, timing, benefits, and risks should be discussed with the relevant physician.

Procedures and adjuncts may support rehabilitation in the right clinical context, but they do not replace the mobility and strengthening work that follows.

## Who this may fit—and what should be checked first

Frozen shoulder most commonly occurs in middle adulthood and is associated with factors including diabetes, thyroid conditions, immobilization, and previous shoulder surgery. If stiffness develops after surgery, the surgeon's protocol takes priority over a general frozen-shoulder timeline.

Other problems should be considered before assuming that shoulder stiffness is adhesive capsulitis. Prompt assessment is appropriate after trauma with an inability to lift the arm, or when there is marked weakness, fever, a hot or swollen joint, unexplained weight loss, a history of cancer with new shoulder pain, severe unrelenting night pain, or neurological symptoms extending down the arm.

Restricted passive motion is an important feature of frozen shoulder. If passive movement is relatively preserved, another diagnosis may be more likely and should be evaluated. Imaging is not always necessary when the clinical pattern is clear, but it may be used when another condition is suspected or the presentation does not fit.

Shockwave, laser, exercise, injections, and other interventions only make sense after the clinical pattern and relevant precautions have been considered.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does frozen shoulder last?

It varies. Symptoms commonly continue for many months, and some people experience a longer course. The stages overlap, so progress is better judged by pain irritability, sleep, function, and usable motion than by a promised end date.

### Can I stretch hard through it?

Forceful stretching is not automatically better. In a highly painful stage, aggressive stretching may increase symptoms. The dose should follow irritability: gentle, tolerable movement when pain is dominant, followed by progressively greater mobility work as the shoulder can tolerate it.

### Can it happen in my other shoulder?

It can, but it is not inevitable. Certain health factors, including diabetes, are associated with a higher risk. New pain or stiffness in the other shoulder should be assessed on its own rather than assumed to follow the same course.

### Do I need an MRI?

Frozen shoulder is often diagnosed from the history and physical examination, including the pattern of restricted passive motion. Imaging may be appropriate when trauma, a tear, arthritis, or another cause is suspected, or when the presentation is unclear. An MRI is not required in every case.

The stage and irritability of the shoulder change what may help. A mobility exercise or treatment that is reasonable in one phase may be too much—or not enough—in another. An assessment can determine whether the pattern fits frozen shoulder, whether pain or stiffness is dominant, and what dose of movement is appropriate now.
