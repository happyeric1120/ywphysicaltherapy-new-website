Tendon pain that has lasted for months creates a real dilemma: a cortisone injection sounds quick, while shockwave therapy sounds slower. But the trade-off between shockwave and cortisone for tendon pain is not about speed alone. It also involves what each option may change, what it leaves unchanged, and the progressive loading work that still needs to happen.

## What tendinopathy actually is

A tendon connects muscle to bone and transmits force. Tendinopathy is commonly understood as a problem involving overload and reduced capacity to adapt to that load. The tendon may become painful and show changes in thickness or collagen organization. Symptoms can appear with gripping, walking, jumping, lifting, or other repeated demands.

Common sites include the lateral elbow, Achilles tendon, patellar tendon, rotator cuff, and gluteal tendons at the side of the hip.

Tendinopathy is not simply an inflammatory problem. That changes how an anti-inflammatory injection should be considered. Cortisone may reduce pain, but it does not by itself rebuild the tendon's ability to tolerate load. Pain relief without a plan to restore capacity addresses only part of the problem.

## How each option works

### Cortisone injection

A cortisone injection places corticosteroid medication at or near the painful area, sometimes with a local anesthetic. Corticosteroids can reduce inflammatory activity and pain. When an injection helps, relief may begin within days. That speed is one reason it may be considered after tendon pain has persisted.

What the injection does not do is directly restore load capacity. A reduction in pain is not the same as a tendon being prepared to tolerate gripping, running, jumping, or overhead activity again. Activity and rehabilitation still need to be progressed appropriately.

### Shockwave therapy

Shockwave therapy delivers mechanical pulses to the treatment area. Focused shockwave directs energy toward a selected depth, while radial shockwave produces pressure waves that are strongest near the applicator and spread through more superficial tissue. Selection depends on the tendon, its depth, the clinical findings, and the treatment plan.

In the clinic, shockwave is best described as a mechanical stimulus rather than a tissue-repair claim. Research primarily measures changes in pain and function; it does not establish that shockwave regenerates a tendon or produces new, orderly collagen.

## What the evidence says

The two options should be compared by timing, later outcomes, tendon site, and individual goals—not by a single success rate.

Cortisone injections may provide faster short-term pain relief for some tendon conditions. The longer-term picture can differ by tendon and diagnosis. For lateral elbow tendinopathy, for example, randomized trials have reported less favorable later outcomes and more recurrence after corticosteroid injection than after some non-injection approaches. Findings from the elbow should not automatically be applied to every tendon, and they do not mean an injection is never appropriate.

When shockwave helps, changes commonly develop over several weeks and are often assessed around 12 weeks rather than after one session. The strength of evidence also varies by body region and diagnosis. Evidence from one condition cannot automatically be transferred to another tendon problem.

Progressive loading remains a central part of tendinopathy rehabilitation. The tendon needs an appropriate level of load, followed by a gradual increase in what it can tolerate. Cortisone or shockwave may change symptoms enough to support that work, but neither performs the rehabilitation for you.

## What treatment may feel like—and the timeline

A cortisone injection is typically performed during one clinical visit. Some people experience a temporary increase in soreness afterward. When an injection reduces pain, the improvement may be noticed relatively quickly, but symptom relief does not establish that the tendon has regained capacity.

Shockwave is commonly delivered as a short series of sessions, although the number and spacing vary. The applicator produces pulses that may feel like strong tapping or a deep ache. Temporary soreness can occur afterward. Any meaningful change is generally assessed over several weeks alongside load management and exercise.

The timing of shockwave, heavier loading, or other procedures after an injection should be decided by the clinicians involved. Practices vary by tendon, injection location, medication, symptoms, and medical history.

## Risks worth knowing

Corticosteroid injections have potential risks. Depending on the medication, location, technique, and individual circumstances, these may include a temporary pain flare, skin color changes, thinning of tissue near the injection site, a temporary increase in blood glucose, infection, or tendon injury. Repeated injections and injection into tendon tissue may raise concern about tendon weakening or rupture.

Common short-term effects of shockwave include discomfort during treatment, redness, bruising, and temporary soreness. More serious complications appear to be uncommon in appropriately selected patients, but individual precautions and risks still need to be reviewed.

Neither option is risk-free, and neither replaces a clear diagnosis.

## Who each option may fit—and who may need another approach

The decision depends on the time horizon, the specific tendon, symptom duration, medical history, and the patient's goals. Cortisone may sometimes be considered when short-term symptom relief is a priority. Shockwave may be considered for certain persistent tendon problems as part of a longer rehabilitation plan. Neither is automatically a first-line treatment for new tendon pain, which is often initially managed with activity modification and guided exercise.

Other problems should be considered before selecting either procedure. A tendon tear is different from tendinopathy. A sudden pop, marked weakness, signs of infection, inflammatory symptoms, unexplained night pain, or unexplained weight loss warrants appropriate medical assessment rather than choosing a procedure based on an article.

Pregnancy, active malignancy in the treatment area, open growth plates, anticoagulant medication, implanted devices near the treatment area, recent injections, and other individual medical factors require careful screening before shockwave therapy. Do not stop or change prescribed medication without guidance from the prescribing clinician.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a cortisone injection bad for my tendon?

Not automatically. A carefully selected and appropriately performed injection may reduce pain for a period of time. Concerns depend on the tendon, injection placement, medication, number of injections, and individual risk factors. Repeated injections or injection into tendon tissue may increase concern about weakening and, rarely, rupture.

### How fast does each option work?

When cortisone helps, pain may improve within days, sometimes after a brief flare. Shockwave generally takes longer to evaluate, with changes assessed over several weeks. A faster decrease in pain does not necessarily mean a more durable result.

### Can I have both?

In some situations both may be considered, but not without a coordinated plan. The appropriate interval between an injection, shockwave, and heavier loading depends on the tendon and individual clinical factors. Using both does not guarantee recovery.

### What if neither works?

The next step is reassessment rather than automatically repeating either intervention. The diagnosis, activity load, rehabilitation program, and other contributing factors may need another look. A clinician can help determine whether imaging, medical referral, or a different rehabilitation plan is appropriate.
